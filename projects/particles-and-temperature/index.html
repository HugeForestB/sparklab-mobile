PART 1 · 2 minutes

1 · Particle motion

Matter is made of tiny particles. Even in a solid, particles move by vibrating near fixed positions. They do not need to travel across the object for it to have thermal energy.

Try it: Slide from cooler to warmer. Watch the particles, then pause.
PART 1 · 2 minutes

2 · Thermal energy

Thermal energy is part of an object’s internal energy associated with its particles’ motion and interactions. For the same substance and state at the same temperature, a sample with more molecules has more thermal energy.

Try it: Change the number of model water molecules in Box B. Boxes A and B stay at 86°F. Compare average motion and the number of molecules.
PART 1 · 2 minutes

3 · Heat: energy in transfer

Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Object A and Object B are equal amounts of the same material. The model keeps their total thermal energy constant while energy transfers from the warmer object to the cooler one.

Try it: Bring Object A and Object B into contact. Follow both temperature readings and the direction of net energy transfer.
PART 1 · 2 minutes

4 · Temperature: a measured property

Temperature is a measurable property that describes the average motion of particles. A temperature sensor exchanges energy with the sample until the sensor reading settles near the sample’s temperature. That reading is not the sample’s total thermal energy.

Try it: Choose a sample, then take its temperature. Watch the sensor reading settle. Repeat with another sample.

REASONING CHALLENGE

Connect the ideas

Connection 1: water and a spoon

A large bowl and a small cup contain the same kind of water at 86°F. A metal spoon at 68°F is placed in the bowl.

Connection 2: ice added to water

A sealed cup contains liquid water at 75°F. Ice at 32°F is added. After several minutes, some ice has melted and the liquid water is cooler.

Ideas to check after writing

The bowl contains more thermal energy than the smaller cup because it contains more of the same substance at the same temperature and state. Energy transfers from the warmer water to the cooler spoon. In the second situation, energy transfers from the warmer liquid water to the colder ice. That energy changes particle motion and arrangement as ice melts. A temperature sensor measures temperature, not total thermal energy. “Cold” does not flow; energy transfers from warmer matter to cooler matter.

Conceptual models: particles, movement, time, and sensor response are simplified; particle size and displacement are not to scale. The contact model uses equal amounts of the same material, conserves their combined thermal energy, and ignores energy exchange with the room. Ice-water reference assumes pure water near ordinary atmospheric pressure.
